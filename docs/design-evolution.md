# Design evolution

## Evidence boundary

This page reflects the distinction described in the project brief. The
prototype screens, design files, and product source needed to verify the
details have not yet been supplied. Descriptions below are therefore a summary
of the brief, not an independent review of either interface.

## What problem was each approach trying to address?

The brief describes an earlier prototype that organized accessibility around
categories of needs and functions. It describes Ultravocal 4.0 as exploring
preference-first personalization for a legal navigation application. Both
approaches are framed around accommodating different access needs; the
specific product problems and interface behaviors require source evidence.

The earlier prototype belonged to a separate application. It must not be
presented as an earlier release or codebase of Ultravocal.

## What assumptions informed the first approach?

The categories and assumptions used by the earlier prototype cannot be
confirmed without its screens or design documentation. It would be
speculative to attribute particular assumptions to the original design.

As a question for review, a needs-category structure may help people find
relevant functions, but it may also make them interpret a category before they
know which setting they need. That is a design hypothesis, not a finding about
the prototype.

## Why explore preference-first personalization?

The brief identifies preference-first personalization as the direction
explored in Ultravocal 4.0. One hypothesis is that choosing interface
preferences directly could offer flexibility without requiring disclosure of
a diagnosis or a fixed identity. Whether the product actually avoids such
disclosure, and whether the choices are understandable and useful, needs
verification in the interface and with users.

## What changed in the structure of the interface?

The intended comparison is between a needs-and-functions organization and
individual preference choices. No authentic screenshots or detailed interface
specifications are available to establish which controls, labels, or flows
changed.

## What trade-offs emerged?

Trade-offs have not been documented in the available materials. Questions to
investigate include whether individual controls improve user agency while
increasing the effort of setup, whether presets aid discovery or constrain
choice, and whether settings remain predictable across different interface
contexts. These are prompts for evidence collection, not reported outcomes.

## What evidence would assess the newer approach?

A responsible comparison would need authentic screens and interaction flows,
reviewed implementation, documented functional checks, and participatory
evaluation with disabled people. Research should examine comprehension,
discoverability, customization, assistive-technology use, and how preferences
work for people with multiple or changing access needs. Neither approach can
be called objectively more accessible without appropriate evidence.
